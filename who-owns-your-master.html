Who Owns Your Master?

The master and the composition are two separate pieces of property. Knowing which one you actually own decides whether you get paid.

Say you record a song. The moment it's finished, there isn't just one thing to "own" — there are two separate pieces of property, and they don't automatically belong to the same person.

The two halves of every song

The composition is the song itself — the melody, the lyrics, the chords. It exists even before anyone presses record. If you wrote the song, you own the composition (or share it with whoever co-wrote it with you).

The master is the actual recording — that specific performance, captured and mixed. A song can be recorded a hundred times by a hundred different artists, and each one creates a brand new master, while the underlying composition stays the same.

Quick way to remember it: the composition is the song on paper. The master is the song in your speakers. Different rights, different owners, different money.

Who owns the master by default

Whoever pays for and controls the recording session usually owns the master — unless a contract says otherwise. This is where things get tricky for Basotho artists working with producers:

  • If you record a song using a beat you leased or licensed from a producer, you typically own the master of your recording — but the producer still owns the underlying beat (the instrumental composition), and your license spells out what you're allowed to do with it.
  • If a studio owner records and produces the whole thing for you without a clear agreement, ownership of the master can get murky fast. This is exactly why a simple written agreement, even a short one, matters more than people think.
  • If you buy an "exclusive" beat license, you're usually getting the rights to use that beat as if you owned the master to it — but "exclusive" doesn't always mean full ownership transfer. Read what the license actually says.

Why the split matters for money

Ownership decides who gets paid when the song is used. Master rights generally earn money from streaming and sales (the "sound recording" royalty), while the composition earns money separately whenever the song is performed, broadcast, or covered by someone else (the "publishing" royalty). If you only own one half, you only get paid for that half.

What to actually do

  1. Before you record with a producer or in a studio, ask directly: "Who owns the master when we're done?" Get the answer in writing, even if it's just a WhatsApp message you both agree to.
  2. Read beat licenses fully before buying — know whether you're leasing (limited rights) or getting a full exclusive transfer.
  3. If you're a producer, decide your own policy upfront: do you keep master rights on leases, and transfer them on exclusives? Say so clearly in every sale.

This guide is educational, not legal advice. Copyright and royalty rules can change, and your specific situation may differ — for anything that affects a real deal or registration, confirm details directly with LESCOSAA or a lawyer.
