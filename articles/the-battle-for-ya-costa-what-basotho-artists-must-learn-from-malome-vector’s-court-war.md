In November 2019, the trajectory of the Basotho music industry shifted forever. Execs from the prominent South African independent label Ambitiouz Entertainment traveled directly to a studio in Maseru, Lesotho, invited by Ntate Stunna to see a rising talent: Bokang Moleli, known to the world as Malome Vector. 



That single studio session birthed the track "1November," a foundation block for his double-platinum debut album, "Karabo". By 2020, Malome Vector had officially relocated to South Africa, signed a major recording contract, and was topping music charts with hits like "Dumelang".



But behind the high-budget music videos and massive radio play lay a heavy business reality. In 2023, the dream turned into a high-stakes legal war in the South African courts. 



\### The Spark: The Dual Release of "Ya Costa"

The industry tension exploded on a Friday in April 2023. Malome Vector, who had recently departed from Ambitiouz Entertainment to launch his independent stable, The Whole Time Entertainment, dropped a highly anticipated single titled "Ya Costa". 



Hours earlier, Ambitiouz Entertainment shockingly released the exact same song, with the exact same artwork, claiming it completely as their own property. 



The label’s argument was strictly legal: because the song’s vocals and beat were initially recorded while Malome Vector was under their contract, they claimed full ownership of the Master recording. Malome Vector's new team countered that they held the local project files and project data, dragging the label to court to fight for his official clearance. 



This dramatic fallout provides three critical legal lessons for independent Basotho artists who want to scale their music beyond Lesotho's borders:



\### Lesson 1: Masters vs. Project Files

A common mistake among new artists is believing that if you physically hold the audio stems or music project files on your laptop, you own the song. Music law doesn't work that way. 



When you sign a traditional record deal, the label funds the studio time and marketing. In exchange, the contract clauses state that the label owns 100% of the "Master" (the final recorded sound). Even if you walk away with the files, you cannot legally distribute, sell, or stream that audio independently without the label’s permission. 



\### Lesson 2: The Trap of Album Deliverables

Many artists assume a contract binds them for a fair timeframe, like "two years." In reality, predatory stables bind you by "Album Deliverables." 



If your contract states you must deliver three albums, you cannot legally leave the company until three full albums are released. If the label delays your recording budget or stalls your marketing, you are legally trapped in limbo—unable to release music independently and unable to exit the deal. Always negotiate for an automatic expiration date (a sunset clause) regardless of album count.



\### Lesson 3: Handshakes Do Not Clear Contracts

Malome Vector’s initial introduction was built on genuine, local industry brotherhood and studio handshakes in Maseru. But brotherhood cannot protect your intellectual property once corporate lawyers step into the room. 



When you decide to part ways with a record label or a management team, packing your bags isn't enough. You must secure a signed "Deed of Release" or a formal "Clearance Certificate." Without this paper, your former label has the legal power to pull your new independent music off Spotify, freeze your live performance royalties, or sue your new distribution partners for breach of contract.



\### The Bottom Line

Malome Vector’s journey proved that Basotho creators have the talent to conquer global markets. But his court battle serves as an urgent warning for \*The Studio Contract\* community: turning a blind eye to contract fine print can freeze your career at its absolute peak. 



Know the business before you sign anything. Protect your masters, understand your deliverables, and always consult an entertainment expert before putting pen to paper.
