According to reporting from Strictly Lesotho Music, the YouTube visualizer for "Makoete" by Malla Shoahla featuring Emchurch has been taken down following a copyright removal request filed by producer Refla Musiq.

## What Refla Musiq is saying

Refla Musiq says he produced the beat used on "Makoete" and had an agreement in place with Malla Shoahla and Emchurch over the record. His account is that once the song came out and started gaining traction, he wasn't credited as the producer — and when he raised it with the artists directly, he wasn't satisfied with the explanation he got back.

## The artists' side, as relayed by Refla Musiq

Per Refla Musiq's account, the response he received pointed to the fact that Malla Shoahla and Emchurch covered the costs of the recording session, the beat, and the video — which he took as their reasoning for leaving him off the credits. Refla Musiq disputes that this settles anything, maintaining that the beat itself still belongs to him regardless of who paid for the studio time around it. After the disagreement went nowhere, he filed the copyright claim that got the visualizer pulled.

Where this stands: Strictly Lesotho Music reports that, as of publishing, Malla Shoahla and Emchurch had not yet responded to Refla Musiq's version of events. This is still developing, and only one side's account is on record so far.

## Why "who paid" and "who owns it" are different questions

Whatever actually happened between these three, the shape of the disagreement is one this site sees constantly. Paying for a studio session, a beat, or a video shoot buys you the right to use those things under whatever terms were agreed — it doesn't automatically transfer ownership of a producer's underlying composition, and it doesn't erase a credit obligation unless a contract said it did.

A short written agreement, signed before the session, settles this before it becomes a public copyright strike. It doesn't need to be complicated: who's credited, who owns what, and what happens if the song blows up. Producers protect themselves by putting their credit and publishing share in writing before they hand over a beat. Artists protect themselves by getting the same thing signed before they build a release around someone else's instrumental.
