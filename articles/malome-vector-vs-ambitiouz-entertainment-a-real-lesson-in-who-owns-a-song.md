\# Malome Vector vs Ambitiouz Entertainment: Who Owns a Song?



For many artists, a song feels personal.



You wrote it. You recorded it. You performed it. You promoted it. Your fans know it as your music.



But in the music business, the biggest question is often not who created the song.



It is:



\> Who owns the legal rights?



The reported dispute involving Lesotho-born artist Malome Vector and Ambitiouz Entertainment is an important lesson for Basotho artists, producers, songwriters and managers.



This article does not say that either side was legally right or wrong. The full contracts and any final legal outcome are not publicly available for us to make that judgment. Instead, it uses the reported disagreement as a practical lesson about music ownership, recording contracts and artist protection.



\## What was publicly reported?



Malome Vector, born Bokang Moleli, was a Lesotho-born rapper and singer who signed with Ambitiouz Entertainment around 2020.



In 2023, South African entertainment outlets reported a disagreement involving a song titled “Ya Costa.” Reports said that Malome Vector released a version of the song through The Whole Time Entertainment while Ambitiouz Entertainment released another version around the same time.



The reports described competing claims about the song and Malome Vector’s contractual status. Some reports said lawyers were involved and that legal action was being considered or pursued.



However, Ambitiouz Entertainment publicly challenged the description of a “court battle,” while Malome Vector reportedly said that his lawyer had advised him not to discuss the matter publicly.



That is why the most responsible lesson is not to declare a winner.



The lesson is this:



\> A single song can involve many different rights, and a contract can decide who controls those rights.



\## A song is not only one thing



When an artist says, “That is my song,” they may be talking about different things.



Music has separate rights, and each right can be owned or controlled by different people.



\## 1. The composition



The composition is the underlying song itself.



It includes:



\- The lyrics

\- The melody

\- The chords

\- The musical structure

\- The songwriter’s contribution



If you write the lyrics and melody, you may own or co-own the composition.



But owning the composition does not automatically mean you own the recording that listeners stream.



\## 2. The master recording



The master is the actual recorded version of the song.



It includes:



\- The vocals

\- The beat

\- The instruments

\- The production

\- The mix and master

\- The final audio file uploaded to streaming platforms



An artist can write a song but still not own the master.



For example, a label may pay for studio time, producers, recording, mixing, videos and marketing. In exchange, the recording contract may give the label ownership of the masters or exclusive control over them.



This means:



\> You can own the words and melody, while someone else owns the recording people listen to.



\## 3. Publishing



Publishing relates to the composition: the lyrics and music.



Publishing income may be earned when a song is:



\- Streamed or downloaded

\- Played on radio

\- Played on television

\- Performed live

\- Used in a movie, advert or game

\- Covered by another artist

\- Sampled by another artist



A publisher or publishing administrator may collect this income on behalf of songwriters.



Before signing a publishing agreement, an artist should understand:



\- What percentage of publishing is being given away?

\- Is it an ownership transfer or only an administration deal?

\- How long does the agreement last?

\- Which countries does it cover?

\- Can the artist approve major uses of the song?



\## 4. Performer, producer and feature rights



A song may also involve other contributors:



\- Producers

\- Beatmakers

\- Featured artists

\- Musicians

\- Vocalists

\- Engineers



A feature does not automatically make someone a co-owner of the song. A producer does not automatically receive a fair share just because they made the beat.



These terms should be agreed in writing before release.



Every session should have clear split sheets, producer agreements and feature agreements.



\## Why two versions of one song can cause a serious dispute



Public reports about “Ya Costa” described different versions of a song with different featured artists.



This matters because a new recording of the same song may create a new master recording.



But whether that new version can legally be released depends on many things:



\- Who owns the composition?

\- Who owns the original master?

\- Was the song created during a label deal?

\- Was it recorded in a label-funded studio?

\- Was the artist still under an exclusive recording contract?

\- Did the producer or featured artists give permission?

\- Does the contract give the label rights over songs made after the artist leaves?



The answer cannot be found only on social media.



The answer is usually found in the contract, studio records, session files, split sheets, release documents and legal notices.



\## What a recording contract can give a label



A label deal is not automatically bad.



A good label can provide:



\- Funding

\- Studio access

\- Music videos

\- Marketing

\- Distribution

\- Playlist pitching

\- Media exposure

\- Industry connections

\- Tour support



But an artist must understand what they are giving in exchange.



A recording contract may give a label:



\- Ownership of master recordings

\- Exclusive rights to record the artist

\- Control over release dates

\- Approval rights over songs and collaborations

\- Rights over unreleased recordings

\- The right to recover its costs before paying royalties

\- Options to extend the contract for more projects

\- Rights to use the artist’s name and image for promotion

\- Rights over recordings made during a period after departure



Never assume that leaving a label means all rights immediately return to the artist.



Read the contract.



\## Ownership versus licensing



Not every music deal has to mean giving away your masters forever.



There are different types of deals.



\### Master assignment



A master assignment means the label becomes the owner of the recordings.



This can be permanent unless the contract says ownership returns to the artist after a certain period.



\### Exclusive licence



An exclusive licence may allow the artist to keep ownership while giving the label exclusive rights to release and exploit the music for a specific period.



After that period, rights may return to the artist.



\### Distribution deal



In a distribution deal, the artist often keeps ownership while a distributor helps deliver music to streaming platforms in exchange for a fee or percentage.



\### Publishing administration deal



A publishing administrator may register songs and collect royalties while the songwriter keeps ownership of the composition.



For independent Basotho artists, keeping ownership wherever possible can build long-term value. But every deal must be judged by what the artist receives in return.



\## Recoupment: why success does not always mean payment



Recoupment means the label recovers agreed costs from the artist’s income before paying royalties.



These costs may include:



\- Studio recording

\- Production

\- Mixing and mastering

\- Music videos

\- Artwork

\- Marketing

\- Promotion

\- Advances

\- Tour support



Recoupment is common in the music business. The problem is when the contract does not clearly explain it.



Before signing, ask:



\- Which costs are recoupable?

\- Is there a spending limit?

\- Must I approve major expenses?

\- Are marketing costs recoupable?

\- Are video costs recoupable?

\- Are label office costs recoupable?

\- Will I owe money personally if the project does not recoup?

\- How often will I receive royalty statements?

\- Can I audit the label’s accounts?



A song can have many streams and still show no artist payment if the account is carrying recoupable costs.



\## Royalties: follow the money



Artists can earn money from different sources.



These may include:



\- Streaming income

\- Downloads

\- Radio royalties

\- Television royalties

\- Live-performance royalties

\- Publishing income

\- Sync licences for adverts, films and games

\- YouTube income

\- Brand partnerships

\- Merchandise

\- Ticket sales

\- Neighbouring rights income



A proper contract should explain:



1. Who collects each income stream.

2. What percentage goes to the artist.

3. What costs can be deducted.

4. When statements are provided.

5. When payments are made.

6. Whether the artist can audit the records.



If you do not understand where the money enters and where it leaves, do not sign yet.



\## Exit and termination clauses



Artists often focus on getting signed.



Smart artists also focus on how they will leave.



A termination clause should answer:



\- How long does the deal last?

\- Is it for a fixed number of years, singles, EPs or albums?

\- Does the label have options to extend the deal?

\- What must happen before the label can extend it?

\- Can the artist leave if the label fails to release music?

\- Can the artist leave if the label does not account or pay?

\- Is there a notice period?

\- What happens to unreleased music?

\- Who owns songs recorded before and after termination?

\- Can the artist re-record old songs?

\- Is there a post-term restriction on new releases?

\- Which country’s law applies if there is a dispute?



A clear exit clause protects both the artist and the label.



\## Questions to ask before signing any music deal



Before you sign, ask these questions:



1. Who will own my masters?

2. Do I keep my songwriting and publishing rights?

3. How long does the deal last?

4. Is the deal exclusive?

5. What exactly must the label provide?

6. What percentage of income will I receive?

7. Which costs are recoupable?

8. Is there a budget cap?

9. When will I get royalty statements?

10. Can I audit the accounts?

11. Who chooses release dates?

12. Who controls collaborations and features?

13. What happens if the label does not release my music?

14. What happens to unfinished songs if I leave?

15. Can I use my stage name and social-media pages after the deal?

16. Can I re-record songs in the future?

17. What happens if there is a disagreement?

18. Have I received independent legal advice?



\## Practical protection for Basotho artists



Before releasing music or sending it to a label, keep your records.



\- Save dated demos, lyrics, stems and session files.

\- Keep producer agreements and beat licences.

\- Agree writing splits before release.

\- Get feature permissions in writing.

\- Register your songs and recordings with the appropriate rights organisations.

\- Keep invoices for studio, production, mastering and video costs.

\- Use written agreements, even with friends.

\- Keep copies of every contract, email and release plan.

\- Never rely only on verbal promises.



A simple split sheet signed after a studio session can prevent a major conflict later.



\## The lesson



The reported Malome Vector and Ambitiouz Entertainment disagreement shows why artists must understand their rights before conflict begins.



The public reports do not provide enough information to decide the legal merits of the dispute. But they do remind every creator of one important truth:



\> Talent creates the music. Documentation protects the music.



Before signing a deal, understand what you own, what you are licensing, what you are assigning, how you will be paid and how you can leave.



That is not distrust.



That is professional protection.
