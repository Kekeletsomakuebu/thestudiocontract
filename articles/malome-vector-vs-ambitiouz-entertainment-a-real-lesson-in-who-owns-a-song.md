Malome Vector, born in Roma, Maseru, was one of the most recognised Basotho voices in South African hip-hop before his death in 2024. Before that, though, his career included a very public, very real example of exactly the kind of contract dispute this Library exists to help creators avoid.



\## How the dispute started



Vector signed with South African label Ambitiouz Entertainment in 2020, going on to release hits including "Dumelang" and "Nobody." About a year before the dispute became public, he left the label, citing neglect and poor marketing as his reasons for going.



\## The fight over "Ya Costa"



The conflict came to a head over a song called "Ya Costa." Vector released the track independently through his own label, The Whole Time Entertainment. According to multiple South African entertainment outlets, Ambitiouz Entertainment released the same song around the same time and claimed ownership of it, arguing Vector had breached his contract by leaving before it ended. Vector, through his legal team, disputed that position and reportedly pursued the matter in court over who actually had the right to release and profit from the song.



Vector wasn't alone in this kind of standoff. Reporting at the time noted Ambitiouz had similar contract disputes with several other artists who left the label under similar circumstances.



\## Why this happens so often



This is the exact tension that shows up throughout the guides in this Library: what a contract says about ownership after an artist leaves a label almost always matters more than what feels fair. If a recording contract gives the label rights over music released within a certain window after departure, a label can have a real legal claim to a song, even one the artist wrote and financed independently after walking away.



For Basotho artists watching this play out from home, the lesson isn't that labels are the enemy. It's that exit terms deserve as much attention as signing terms. Before signing with any label, know exactly what happens to your future work if you ever decide to leave, because by the time that question matters, it's usually too late to renegotiate it.



Malome Vector passed away in July 2024. His catalogue, and the questions his career raised about ownership and fairness in the industry, remain part of the conversation this Library is built around.
