# **Why this matters for Basotho artists**

The reported disagreement involving Malome Vector and Ambitiouz Entertainment is a useful lesson for every artist, producer, songwriter and manager in Lesotho.

It is not a lesson about choosing sides. It is a lesson about understanding contracts before signing them.

A song may feel like it belongs to the person who wrote and performed it. However, in the music business, ownership can be divided between the artist, songwriter, producer, publisher, label and distributor.

**What was publicly reported**

Malome Vector, born Bokang Moleli, was a Lesotho-born rapper and singer who signed with Ambitiouz Entertainment around 2020.

In 2023, South African entertainment outlets reported a disagreement relating to a song titled “Ya Costa.” Reports stated that Malome Vector released a version through The Whole Time Entertainment, while Ambitiouz Entertainment released another version around the same time.

The reports described disagreement about the song and Malome Vector’s contractual position. Some reports referred to lawyers and possible legal action.

However, Ambitiouz Entertainment publicly questioned the description of a court battle. Malome Vector reportedly said that his lawyer had advised him not to discuss the matter publicly.

For that reason, this article does not claim that either party was legally right or wrong. The full agreement between the parties, as well as any final legal outcome, is not publicly available for this Library to decide that question.

The important lesson is simple: a song can have more than one owner, and a contract can decide who controls which rights.

**A song is made up of different rights**

When an artist says, “That is my song,” they may mean different things. Music is not just one piece of property.

**1. The composition**

The composition is the underlying musical work. It includes the lyrics, melody, chords, musical structure and songwriting contribution.

If you wrote the lyrics and melody, you may own or co-own the composition. However, owning the composition does not automatically mean you own the recorded version that people stream.

**2. The master recording**

The master is the actual recorded audio file. It includes the vocals, beat, instruments, production, mix, master and final version uploaded to streaming platforms.

An artist can write and perform a song but still not own its master recording.

For example, a label may pay for studio time, producers, mixing, mastering, videos and promotion. In exchange, the recording contract may give the label ownership of the master or exclusive rights to control it.

You can own the lyrics and melody while another person or company owns the recording that fans hear.

**3. Publishing rights**

Publishing relates to the composition: the lyrics and music.

Publishing income can be earned when a song is streamed, downloaded, played on radio or television, performed live, used in an advert or film, covered, or sampled.

Before signing a publishing agreement, ask:

1. Am I giving away ownership, or only allowing someone to administer my songs?
2. What percentage of publishing income will I receive?
3. How long will the agreement last?
4. Which countries does the agreement cover?
5. Do I have to approve major uses of my songs?

**4. Producer, feature and performer rights**

A recording may involve producers, beatmakers, featured artists, session musicians, vocalists and engineers.

A featured artist is not automatically a co-owner of the master or composition. A producer is also not automatically protected simply because they made the beat.

Important terms should be agreed in writing before release. This includes writing splits, producer fees, producer royalty points, feature fees, permission to use contributions and credits.

# **Why two versions of one song can create a problem**

Public reporting about “Ya Costa” described different versions of the song with different featured artists.

A new recording of the same underlying song can create a new master recording. However, that does not always mean it can be released without a dispute.

The answer may depend on:

1. Who owns the composition.
2. Who owns the original master.
3. When the song was written.
4. Where the song was recorded.
5. Who paid for the recording.
6. Whether the artist was still under an exclusive recording contract.
7. Whether producers and featured artists gave written permission.
8. Whether the contract gives the label rights over music created after the artist leaves.

Social-media posts do not decide these questions. The contract, studio records, session files, split sheets and written permissions are what matter.

**What a recording contract can give a label**

A record deal is not automatically a bad deal. A good label can provide funding, studio support, music videos, marketing, distribution, media exposure and industry connections.

But artists must understand what they are giving in return.

A recording contract may give a label ownership of masters recorded during the contract period, exclusive rights to record the artist, control over release dates, approval rights over songs and collaborations, rights over unreleased recordings, and the right to recover certain costs before paying royalties.

Never assume that leaving a label means every right immediately returns to you. Read the agreement carefully.

**Ownership and licensing are different**

Not every deal requires an artist to give away their masters forever.

A master assignment means the label becomes the owner of the master recording. This may be permanent unless the contract says the rights return later.

An exclusive licence means the artist may keep ownership but gives the label exclusive rights to release and exploit the music for a specific period.

A distribution deal often allows the artist to keep ownership while a distributor delivers the music to streaming platforms for a fee or percentage.

A publishing administration deal can allow the songwriter to keep ownership of the composition while an administrator registers songs and collects royalties for a commission.

Before agreeing to any deal, ask clearly: “Will I own my masters, or am I licensing them to you?”

**Recoupment: why streams do not always mean payment**

Recoupment means the label recovers agreed costs from the artist’s income before paying royalties.

These costs may include recording, production, mixing and mastering, music videos, artwork, marketing, promotion, advances and tour support.

Recoupment is common in the industry. The danger is unclear recoupment.

Before signing, ask:

1. Which costs are recoupable?
2. Is there a limit on what can be spent?
3. Must I approve major spending?
4. Are marketing and video costs recoupable?
5. Can I personally owe money if the project does not recoup?
6. How often will I receive royalty statements?
7. Can I audit the label’s accounts?

A song can have many streams while an artist receives no royalty payment if recoupable costs are still being recovered.

**Royalties: know where the money goes**

Artists can earn income from streaming, downloads, radio, television, live performances, publishing, sync licences, YouTube, brand partnerships, merchandise, ticket sales and neighbouring rights.

A proper agreement should explain:

1. Who collects each type of income.
2. What percentage belongs to the artist.
3. What costs can be deducted.
4. When statements will be issued.
5. When payments will be made.
6. Whether the artist can inspect or audit the records.

If you cannot follow the money on paper, do not sign the deal yet.

**Exit and termination clauses**

Artists often focus on getting signed. They should also focus on how they can leave.

A good termination clause should answer:

1. How long does the agreement last?
2. Is it for a fixed period, singles, EPs or albums?
3. Does the label have options to extend the agreement?
4. Can the artist leave if the label does not release music?
5. Can the artist leave if the label does not account or pay?
6. What happens to unreleased songs?
7. Who owns music recorded before and after termination?
8. Can the artist re-record older songs?
9. Is there a restriction on releasing music after leaving?
10. Which country’s law applies if there is a disagreement?

**Questions every artist should ask before signing**

1. Who owns my master recordings?
2. Do I keep my songwriting and publishing rights?
3. How long is the contract?
4. Is the deal exclusive?
5. What exactly will the label provide?
6. What percentage of income will I receive?
7. Which costs are recoupable?
8. Is there a spending limit?
9. When will I receive royalty statements?
10. Can I audit the accounts?
11. Who controls release dates?
12. What happens if the label does not release my music?
13. What happens to unfinished songs if I leave?
14. Can I re-record songs in the future?
15. Have I received independent legal advice?

**Practical protection for Basotho artists**

Before you release music or send it to a label, protect your work.

1. Save dated demos, lyrics, stems and session files.
2. Keep written producer agreements and beat licences.
3. Agree songwriting splits before release.
4. Get feature permissions in writing.
5. Register songs and recordings with the appropriate rights organisations.
6. Keep invoices for studio, production, mastering and video costs.
7. Use written agreements, even when working with friends.
8. Keep copies of every contract, email and release plan.
9. Do not rely only on verbal promises.

A simple signed split sheet after a studio session can prevent a serious disagreement later.

# **The main lesson**

The reported Malome Vector and Ambitiouz Entertainment dispute shows why artists must understand their rights before conflict begins.

The public reports do not provide enough information to decide the legal outcome. But they remind every creator of an important truth:

Talent creates the music. Documentation protects the music.

Before signing, understand what you own, what you are licensing, what you are assigning, how you will be paid and how you can leave.

That is not distrust. That is professional protection.
