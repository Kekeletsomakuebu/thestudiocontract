Master vs. Publishing

Ownership tells you who holds the rights. This guide is about the other half of the question — where the money each right earns actually comes from, and why one song quietly pays out twice.

Every song that gets played, streamed, or performed is earning two separate kinds of royalties at the same time, tied to two separate rights. If you don't know both exist, it's easy to only ever collect one of them — and never notice the other has been sitting uncollected the whole time.

Two rights, two royalty streams

This picks up where Who Owns Your Master? leaves off. Once you know the master and the composition are separate pieces of property, the next step is knowing each one generates its own income:

  • Master royalties are earned by the sound recording — the specific take that got mixed and released. These go to whoever owns the master: usually the artist, a label, or sometimes a producer, depending on the deal.
  • Publishing royalties are earned by the composition — the song underneath the recording. These go to the songwriters and whoever administers or publishes their work, regardless of which recording of the song gets used.
Why it feels invisible: streaming platforms and radio stations pay both royalty streams, but usually through different pipes, to different registered parties. If your publishing was never registered anywhere, that half of the money has nowhere to go — it doesn't wait for you, it just goes uncollected or gets absorbed elsewhere.

Where publishing money actually comes from

Publishing royalties break down into a few types, and Basotho creators will run into all of them:

  • Performance royalties — generated whenever a song is played publicly: radio, TV, live venues, streaming. Collection societies are supposed to track these plays and pay songwriters accordingly.
  • Mechanical royalties — generated whenever a composition is reproduced, historically through physical copies, now mostly through streams and downloads.
  • Sync royalties — generated when a song is licensed into a film, ad, series, or game. These are negotiated directly and tend to pay a flat fee rather than a running royalty.

Master royalties are simpler by comparison — they're the "sound recording" side of streaming and sales payouts, and they go wherever the master ownership says they go.

Where producers fit into this

This is where beat deals get complicated. A producer's beat is itself a composition — so when an artist records over a leased or purchased beat, the producer is very often owed a publishing share as a co-writer of the underlying composition, on top of whatever the beat license already covers for the master side. Two common ways this goes wrong:

  • An artist assumes buying a beat "clears" everything, including publishing, when the license only ever covered the recording use.
  • A producer never gets registered as a co-writer anywhere, so even when the song earns performance or mechanical royalties, there's no paper trail sending any of it their way.

Neither side is necessarily acting in bad faith — most of the time, nobody wrote anything down, and the split sheet just never happened.

What to actually do

  1. Every time a song has more than one contributor — writer, topliner, producer — fill out a split sheet the same day, while everyone agrees and remembers who did what.
  2. Register your compositions with a collection society so performance and mechanical royalties have somewhere to be paid.
  3. If you're a producer, decide upfront whether your beat sales include a writer's share of publishing, and put that in the agreement — don't leave it to be assumed later.
  4. Keep master rights and publishing rights on separate lines in every contract. They're two different assets, and lumping them into one vague clause is how one side ends up with nothing.

This guide is educational, not legal advice. Copyright and royalty rules can change, and your specific situation may differ — for anything that affects a real deal or registration, confirm details directly with LESCOSAA or a lawyer.
