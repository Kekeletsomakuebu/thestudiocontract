Copyright Basics: Protecting Your Work

Copyright protection starts the moment you create something — no paperwork required. Here's what that actually means for you.

Here's the part that surprises a lot of creators: you don't have to do anything for copyright to exist. The moment you finish writing a song, recording a verse, or producing a beat, copyright protection already applies — automatically, under both Lesotho law and international copyright treaties Lesotho is part of.

What copyright actually protects

Copyright protects your expression of an idea, not the idea itself. Two producers can both have the idea "amapiano beat with a gospel sample" — that idea alone isn't protected. But the actual beat one of them makes, once it exists in a fixed form (recorded, saved, written down), is protected the second it's created.

This covers musical works, lyrics, recordings, and more — as long as the work is original and exists in some tangible form, not just in your head.

Do you need to register it?

Under Lesotho's Copyright Order, protection does not depend on registration — it exists automatically once a qualifying work is created. That said, having a documented, dated record of your work (a saved project file, an email to yourself, a registered deposit) makes it far easier to prove you created something first if a dispute ever comes up. Think of registration less as "turning on" your rights, and more as building evidence in case you ever need it.

Who owns it by default: generally, the person who created the work owns the copyright. The one common exception is if you created something as an employee in the course of your job — then it can belong to your employer unless you agreed otherwise. This matters if you're producing for a studio as staff versus as an independent producer.

What copyright lets you actually do

  • Control who can reproduce, distribute, or publicly perform your work
  • License your work to others — fully or partially, for money or for free
  • Take action if someone uses your work without permission

Owning a physical or digital copy of something — a downloaded beat, a CD, an mp3 — is not the same as owning the copyright to it. You own the file. The creator still owns the rights, unless those rights were specifically transferred to you.

What to actually do the moment you finish a song

  1. Save a dated, backed-up copy somewhere reliable — this becomes your evidence of when the work was created.
  2. If you collaborated, agree on a split sheet immediately, while everyone remembers who contributed what.
  3. Register with LESCOSAA so your work is officially in the system tied to your name — this connects your copyright to the royalty collection process covered in How Royalties Actually Reach You.
  4. Before signing anything that involves your song — a license, a distribution deal, a producer agreement — know which rights you're keeping and which you're giving away.

This guide is educational, not legal advice. Copyright and royalty rules can change, and your specific situation may differ — for anything that affects a real deal or registration, confirm details directly with LESCOSAA or a lawyer.
